01

From Classification to Detection

You already know image classification. Seeing exactly what it can't do tells us what every part of YOLO is for.

In classification, an image flows through a CNN; the spatial feature map is pooled into a single vector, a softmax produces class probabilities, and you get one label — it answers "what is in this image?"

Detection must answer three things at once — what, where, and how many.

Classification

Whole image → CNN → one answer. No location, no count.

Detection (the goal)

Same image → a box + label for every object.

The name YOLO — "You Only Look Once" means it produces all detections in a single forward pass, instead of the older two-stage approach (propose regions, then classify each). That is what makes it fast.

1
Key idea — Classification gives one answer for the whole image; detection needs an answer for every object.

The Core Idea — Classification on a Grid

One insight turns a classifier into a detector. Get this and the rest is detail.

A classifier throws away the feature map's spatial layout — it pools [C, H, W] into one vector. YOLO's move: keep the H×W grid, and treat every cell as its own tiny prediction — a class plus a box for whatever object overlaps that cell.

A cell's center mapped to image coordinates is its anchor point — a point, not a box. YOLO26 is anchor-free: each cell regresses a box directly, with no predefined anchor boxes.

The Skeleton — Backbone · Neck · Head

Before going deeper, here is the whole network in one picture. Every YOLO has three parts. Right now, focus on the Backbone.

Highlighted: how the Backbone produces feature maps. The Neck and Head come in Acts 5–8.

The Backbone is just a CNN — stacked convolutions, downsampling, growing channels. Each stride-2 convolution halves the spatial size, producing a sequence of ever-smaller feature maps:

Full sequence P1–P5. YOLO taps P3, P4, P5 — and skips P1/P2 (see below).

The backbone produces five levels, but YOLO only taps three: P3 (80²), P4 (40²), P5 (20²). Why not P1/P2? They are too shallow — their features are still low-level edges with weak semantics, and their grids are so fine (320², 160²) that using them would be expensive for little gain. P3–P5 hit the sweet spot.

P3 = 640 ÷ 8 = 80²  ·  P4 = 640 ÷ 16 = 40²  ·  P5 = 640 ÷ 32 = 20²
3
Key idea — Backbone, Neck, Head. The backbone makes P1–P5; YOLO uses P3/P4/P5 — the sweet spot of resolution and semantics.
04

Receptive Field — Why Three Scales

Why three feature maps instead of one? Because one cell on each level "sees" a different-sized region when you project it back onto the input image.

The receptive field (RF) of a cell is the region of the input image that affects it. The 3D view below makes it literal: pick a cell on the feature plane, and the cone projects down to the patch of the input image it actually sees.

Level: Object:

Deeper, coarser levels (P5) have a large RF — good for big objects. Shallow, fine levels (P3) have a small RF — good for small objects. Project a cell back and the rule is obvious: an object must fit inside the RF (or a cell sees only a fragment) and be larger than one cell (or it is lost in the grid). That is exactly why three scales exist.

▸ New in YOLO26

RF is a property of the convolutional backbone — 26 doesn't change it. The SPPF and C2PSA blocks at the end of the backbone deliberately widen P5's receptive field for large objects and scene context.

4
Key idea — A cell only detects what fits inside its receptive field — so each scale is tuned to a range of object sizes.
05

The Neck — Fusing the Three Scales

We know why three scales exist. The Neck mixes them — here is the whole thing in one picture, drawn together with the Backbone so you can see where every map comes from.

The problem. P3 (shallow) has sharp location but weak semantics. P5 (deep) has strong semantics but coarse location. Neither is good alone — so the Neck runs two flows.

⊕ = Concat (fuse) · the vertical axis is resolution / depth — P3 shallow & fine at the bottom, P5 deep & coarse at the top.

Read it left to right. The Backbone (faint, left) goes deep bottom-to-top and taps three levels — C3, C4, C5. The FPN — top-down flow upsamples deep maps and merges them downward via lateral connections (carrying semantics down). The PAN — bottom-up flow then downsamples and merges upward (carrying precise localization up). Each ⊕ is a Concat + a conv block that learns to mix. Three fused maps leave to the Head (right).

▸ Worth noting

The two flows don't connect level-for-level blindly: PAN-P5 is fused with the Backbone's C5 directly (not the FPN-P5 output), PAN-P4 takes the FPN-P4 result, and PAN-P3 simply is the FPN-P3 output. The diagram draws these exact connections.

5
Key idea — The Neck sends meaning downward (FPN) and precision upward (PAN), so every output scale has both.

The Grid & Ground Truth — How It Learns

Training only

Each Head turns a feature map into a prediction grid. Here is what a cell holds — and how it learns from ground-truth boxes.

The Head's output is a tensor: a grid of cells, and along the channel axis each cell carries a prediction vector — 4 box numbers plus one score per class. (What those 4 numbers really are is explained right after.) Tap a cell:

The Head output is a 3D tensor — H×W grid × (4 + classes) channels. Each cell's channel column is its prediction.

What the 4 box channels really are — l, t, r, b

It is tempting to call the 4 box numbers "x, y, w, h", but YOLO26's anchor-free head does not regress a center and size. Each cell predicts 4 distances from its own anchor point to the four edges of the box — l, t, r, b (left, top, right, bottom). Distances from a fixed point are easier to learn and pair naturally with the box loss.

These raw distances are not pixels and not 0–1 — they are in cell units. To become a real box they are decoded with the cell's anchor point and the level's stride. Drag the four sliders and watch the box — and its decoded pixel coordinates — update live:

Decode for level

Raw l, t, r, b stay the same — only the × stride factor changes.

Notice the raw distances never change when you switch level — only the × stride step does. So the chain is: raw output = cell-unit distances → × stride → 640 px → (undo letterbox) → original image. That last decode step is shown in Act 8.

Mapping a ground-truth box to cells

During training every image has ground-truth (GT) boxes. The model must decide which cell is responsible for which GT box — this is label assignment.

First: put the GT box and the grid in the same coordinates

The GT box from the label file is in original-image pixels; the grid is in cell units. The two cannot be compared until they are in the same units.

The journey from one to the other has two distinct stages, and they are different kinds of transform:

  • Letterbox — the original image (any size, any aspect ratio) is scaled by a single factor r and grey-padded to 640 × 640. Because every axis uses the same r, the box keeps its shape — it is resized and shifted, never stretched.
  • ÷ stride — the 640 px box is divided by the level's stride (8, 16, 32) to land in cell units. This is not a resize at all; it just relabels the same picture in a coarser unit.

So the full path is original px → (letterbox: × r, pad) → 640 px → (÷ stride) → cell units. And it is reversible: run it backward — × stride, then undo the letterbox — and you have exactly the decode step from the previous section. Same transform, opposite directions; both bring the predicted box and the GT box into a shared coordinate system so assignment can compare them.

The chain below runs the conversion either way — pick a direction and a box size, and watch the same box travel between original pixels, 640 px, and the three grids:

Direction: Box size: Source grid:

One GT box covers many cells on P3 but only a few — or none — on P5. That is the training-side echo of the receptive-field story from Act 4: a small object barely registers on the coarse grids, which is why it must be caught on P3. Either way, once the box is in cell units, checking "is a cell's anchor point inside it?" is just a number comparison.

How many cells? 8400

So far we've looked at one cell on one grid. Across all three grids the cells add up to a number you'll keep seeing — 8400 — and it is not magic:

Where do a cell's predictions come from?

One last thing before assignment. Every cell already outputs that channel column — the 4 distances l, t, r, b plus a score per class — on every forward pass, trained or not. Early in training those numbers are basically random, but they exist. So a cell always has a predicted box and predicted class scores. Label assignment runs on top of these raw predictions — it never comes before them.

The three steps of assignment

With the box on the grid, assignment runs in three steps:

  • ① Filter candidates — only cells whose anchor point falls inside the converted GT box compete; every other cell is immediately background. The cells of all three grids are pooled into one set first, so a candidate may come from P3, P4 or P5 — assignment never runs one grid at a time. This step is pure geometry — it never looks at the model's weights.
  • ② Score each candidate with the alignment metric t = sα·uβ. Both values are read straight from that cell's channel column: s is its predicted score for the GT's class, and u is the IoU between its predicted box (the l, t, r, b channels, decoded) and the GT box.
  • ③ Take top-k — rank candidates by t; the best k become positive (trained toward this GT), the rest become background.

▸ One pool, not three

Before assignment, the three grids are flattened and concatenated into a single set of 8400 cells (80² + 40² + 20²). Steps ①–③ run once over that whole pool — so a single GT box can collect positive cells from more than one scale at once (some from P3, some from P4). Nothing routes an object to a fixed grid; which scale ends up winning is an emergent effect of the u (IoU) term — a cell only scores well when its decoded box fits the GT, which happens naturally at the scale that matches the object's size.

Where does s come from?

The GT box carries a class label (say, "car"). The cell's channel column has one score per class. s is simply the value in the channel that matches the GT's label — not all of them, not the argmax, just that one indexed channel. It is a lookup into the tensor, nothing more:

▸ The chicken-and-egg question

If the weights start random, the predicted boxes are garbage — so what does assignment align to on step 0? The answer is step ①. Filtering candidates is pure geometry: it depends only on whether a cell sits inside the GT box, never on the weights. So even at random initialization, assignment still picks cells in the right region. On step 0 every candidate's t is tiny and roughly equal, so top-k is almost a coin flip within that correct region — and the chosen cells get pulled toward the GT by the loss. Next step their t is a little higher, so ② starts selecting them because they are good. Step ① is the anchor that keeps the system from drifting; ② and ③ only sharpen with training. After assignment: computing the loss

Once every cell is labelled positive or background, the loss is computed — and the two kinds of cell are trained differently:

Positive cells get two losses: a box loss (CIoU) pulling their decoded l, t, r, b box toward the GT box, and a class loss (BCE) pushing the GT class channel up. Background cells get only the class loss (BCE) — pushing every class channel down toward "nothing" — and no box loss, since there is no box to match. The losses are summed and backpropagation updates the whole network. Those updates are exactly what make the next forward pass's boxes and scores a little better — which is why assignment sharpens over training.

It is worth being precise about those two losses — and a picture helps more than the names do.

The box loss is CIoU, not a coordinate-wise regression. A plain L1 loss would score each of the four distances l, t, r, b on its own; CIoU instead scores the predicted box against the GT box as one whole shape, combining three parts:

CIoU loss
= (1 − IoU) + d²/c² + α·v

where

d distance between the two box centres
c diagonal of the smallest box that encloses both boxes
v (4/π²)·(arctan(w_gt/h_gt) − arctan(w/h))² — aspect-ratio mismatch, 0 when the ratios match
α v / ((1−IoU) + v) — trade-off weight, stays small until the boxes start to overlap

Because IoU is a ratio it is scale-invariant — and the centre-distance term keeps a useful gradient even when the two boxes do not overlap at all, where a raw IoU loss would be flat at zero.

The class loss is BCE — binary cross-entropy on each class channel independently, each with its own sigmoid (no shared softmax). Every channel is just pulled toward a simple 0 or 1 target:

Class channel bird car · GT class person dog
Predicted score · own sigmoid, 0–1 0.28 0.52 0.16 0.44
Target — positive cell · GT = car 0 1 0 0
Target — background cell · no object 0 0 0 0
Each row is a target vector — BCE pushes every predicted score toward its column's target, up toward 1 or down toward 0.
BCE loss · per cell
= − Σc [ y·log(p) + (1−y)·log(1−p) ]

where

p predicted score for the channel — its own sigmoid, 0–1
y target for the channel — 1 for the GT class of a positive cell, 0 otherwise
Σc runs over every class channel — each is an independent binary decision, never coupled by a softmax

A positive cell pulls its GT-class channel up and the rest down; a background cell pulls every channel down — which is how a cell learns to predict "nothing" without needing a dedicated background class. One refinement: modern YOLO does not use a hard 1 for the positive class — it uses the normalised alignment score t as a soft target, so a well-aligned cell is trained toward ~1 and a barely-qualifying one toward less.

Two heads — how YOLO26 drops NMS

Everything so far described one assignment rule — top-k, many cells per GT. That is YOLO26's one-to-many head. But a model trained only that way produces many overlapping boxes per object at inference, which then need NMS to clean up. YOLO26 avoids NMS by training a second head alongside it:

Both heads read the same backbone + neck features — they differ only in label assignment. The one-to-many head assigns the top-k cells (dense supervision — this is what trains the shared features well). The one-to-one head assigns only the single top-1 cell to each GT and pushes every neighbouring cell toward background (score 0) — so it learns to be decisive: one confident cell per object, the rest near zero. No filtering step ever runs; the decisiveness is baked into the weights during training. (This is the "consistent dual assignment" idea from YOLOv10.)

▸ New in YOLO26

This dual-head design is why YOLO26 is end-to-end and NMS-free. The one-to-one head's strict top-1 assignment removes duplicate detections by training, not by a post-processing step.

08

From Grid to Box — The Final Answer

Inference

A trained model runs forward only. Here is how the raw grids become a clean set of boxes on the original image.

Every cell across P3/P4/P5 outputs a vector — 8400 in total. Its 4 box channels are the l, t, r, b distances from Act 7; they are decoded into pixel coordinates with the cell's anchor index and the level's stride — x1 = (col − l) × stride, and likewise for the other three edges. Older YOLO heads produce many overlapping boxes per object, so they need NMS — Non-Maximum Suppression: a post-processing step that keeps the highest-confidence box and removes overlapping duplicates.

▸ New in YOLO26

At inference YOLO26 runs only the one-to-one head — the one-to-many head from Act 7 is simply not executed. Its job finished during training: it gave the shared backbone and neck dense supervision so they learned good features. Those features are now trained, so running the one-to-many head would only add duplicate boxes — it is dropped at zero cost. The one-to-one head alone outputs one confident box per object, so no NMS is needed: YOLO26 is end-to-end and NMS-free.

Finally, because the input was letterboxed, the coordinates are mapped back through the letterbox transform (undo the scale and padding) to land on the original image.

8
Key idea — 8400 raw predictions are decoded, deduplicated and mapped back — and YOLO26 deduplicates without NMS.

Training vs inference

Training

Forward pass → label assignment → loss (BCE + CIoU) → backpropagation. Label assignment runs only here.

Inference

Forward pass only → decode boxes → one-to-one head (no NMS) → map back through letterbox → final boxes.
